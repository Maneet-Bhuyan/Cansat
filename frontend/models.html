Autonomous Flight Intelligence · 6 Specialized Models

The AI & predictive models powering the CanSat

A high-speed rocket flight produces noisy, fluctuating sensor data. Instead of relying on rigid hardcoded formulas or slow human commands from the ground, the Cognitive CanSat utilizes an integrated suite of six specialized machine learning models. Together, they give the probe real-time sight, spatial reflexes, self-calibration, and predictive landing safety.

COMPUTATIONAL ARCHITECTURE

Two Brains: Onboard Reflexes & Ground Intelligence

Why don't all models run in one place? Radio waves travel with latency and microcontrollers have strict memory limits. The system strategically divides the computing workload into two domains:

Domain 1 // In The Air

Onboard Edge Microcontroller (ESP32-CAM)

Runs ultra-compact neural networks directly inside 520 KB of internal chip memory at 240 MHz. Because there is no internet connection and no time to wait for a radio signal, this model acts like the human spinal reflex: it sees danger and steers instantly in 38 milliseconds.

Primary Model: TinyLandingNet (Hazard Vision)
Domain 2 // On The Ground

Ground Station Telemetry Engine (Mission Control)

Runs heavier predictive models on the ground station laptop receiving the 433MHz radio stream. These models perform deep sensor physics calibration, detect hidden in-flight anomalies, predict the exact second of landing, and forecast peak apogee altitude.

Models: Flight Phase, Countdown (TTD), Physics Calibrator, Apogee, Anomaly
DETAILED MODEL BREAKDOWN

The 6 Core Models Explained

Every model serves a specific purpose in ensuring the CanSat survives its descent, understands its environment, and lands safely.

Model 01 · Edge Computer Vision Onboard Airframe

TinyLandingNet: Downward Hazard Vision

Plain English Analogy
The CanSat's eyes and visual reflexes. Like looking out of a falling plane, recognizing whether you are over trees, roads, or grass, and pointing the steering parachute toward safe grass before you hit the ground.

What it does: Analyzes raw camera images captured directly underneath the descending CanSat at 10 frames per second. It splits the ground view into a 3×3 grid (9 sectors) and classifies each sector into one of four terrain types:

  • SAFE_LZ: Open grassy fields, soft soil, or agricultural farmland suitable for impact.
  • OBSTACLE_CANOPY: Dense trees and forest cover that could snag the parachute.
  • CRITICAL_HAZARD: Concrete buildings, roofs, active roads, or high-voltage power lines.
  • WATER_HAZARD: Rivers, lakes, or swamps that would drown electronics upon touchdown.

What it predicts: The safest landing sector and an evasion heading angle (e.g. 315° northwest) to steer the parachute lines autonomously.

Model 02 · State Estimation Ground Station

Flight Phase & State Classifier

Plain English Analogy
The automated flight director. Rocket launches happen too quickly for humans to press buttons. This model watches all sensors at once like an experienced pilot to know exactly when the rocket stops climbing and starts falling.

What it does: Watches 17 incoming telemetry variables (barometric altitude, rate of climb/fall, 3-axis acceleration, 3-axis gyroscope spin, and battery voltage) to identify what stage of flight the probe is currently in.

What it predicts: Identifies 5 critical flight phases:

  • PAD_IDLE (100% recall): Sitting on the launchpad before ignition.
  • BALLOON_ASCENT (99.7% recall): High-speed climbing through the atmosphere.
  • APOGEE_BURST: The precise moment upward velocity hits zero at maximum height.
  • PARACHUTE_DESCENT (98.1% recall): Steady downward float under canopy.
  • TOUCHDOWN_RECOVERY (100% recall): Impact detected and resting on the ground.
Algorithm
Random Forest Classifier

98.56%

Model 03 · Descent Analytics Ground Station

Touchdown Prognostics Regressor

Plain English Analogy
The smart landing countdown clock. Simply dividing height by speed gives false estimates because air gets thicker near the ground and wind causes swinging. This model constantly updates an accurate countdown clock so recovery crews know the exact second it touches down.

What it does: Learns the nonlinear aerodynamic drag profile of the parachute as it descends through changing atmospheric air density (from $1.10\,\text{kg/m}^3$ at high altitude to $1.225\,\text{kg/m}^3$ at sea level).

What it predicts: Time-to-Touchdown (TTD in seconds) with statistical confidence bounds:

  • P10 (Fastest Case): If heavy downward wind shears accelerate the fall.
  • P50 (Expected Case): The most probable landing second.
  • P90 (Slowest Case): If thermal updrafts keep the parachute lofted longer.
Algorithm
Multi-Quantile GBDT
Confidence Bounds
10th, 50th, 90th Percentile
Target Metric
Time-to-Touchdown (s)

Air density drag curve
Model 04 · Physics Calibration Ground Station

Sensor Physics Calibrator

Plain English Analogy
The wind-rush filter. When you stick your hand out of a fast-moving car window, rushing air creates false pressure. The CanSat's altitude sensor experiences the same error while falling. This model subtracts the wind distortion to calculate the probe's true height and speed.

What it does: High-speed descent creates aerodynamic pressure drops (Bernoulli effect) that fool barometers into reporting incorrect altitudes. Furthermore, onboard electronics generate heat that causes temperature drift in MEMS sensors.

What it predicts: Takes 13 raw noisy sensor channels and outputs:

  • True Geometric Altitude: Error reduced from $\pm 18\,\text{m}$ down to $0.49\,\text{m}$.
  • True Vertical Speed: Cleaned of aerodynamic buffeting and vibration noise.

Model 05 · Trajectory Forecasting Ground Station

Apogee Peak Altitude Regressor

Plain English Analogy
The altitude forecaster. Just like a basketball player knows whether a shot will reach the hoop the moment it leaves their fingers, this model watches the first few seconds of the rocket burn and calculates exactly how high it will soar before falling.

What it does: Analyzes early rocket motor burn dynamics, thrust acceleration curves, and atmospheric pressure decay during the initial 5 to 10 seconds of launch.

What it predicts: The maximum peak altitude (apogee in meters AGL) the rocket will attain before it starts falling. This enables mission controllers to know if the launch will clear competition flight ceilings or drift outside safety perimeters.

Model 06 · Safety & Health Ground Station

In-Flight Telemetry Anomaly Detector

Plain English Analogy
The onboard flight doctor. It has studied hundreds of healthy flight patterns. If something unexpected happens—like a sudden tumble, severe battery drain, or a loose sensor cable—it sounds the alarm on Mission Control instantly.

What it does: High-g launches can cause solder joints to fracture, parachutes to tangle, or radio packets to corrupt. Instead of requiring engineers to watch 17 graphs simultaneously, this unsupervised model continuously evaluates all telemetry in high-dimensional space.

What it predicts: A continuous Anomaly Health Score (0.0 normal to 1.0 critical hazard). If the score crosses the 0.06 contamination threshold, it highlights the exact sensor channel that is misbehaving.
